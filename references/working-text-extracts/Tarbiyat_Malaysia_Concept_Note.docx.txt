TARBIYAT MALAYSIA
National Youth Tech Missions School & College
Malaysia already has schools, universities and technology institutions. This proposal is for something different: a school-age-to-college innovation system where young Malaysians do not merely learn to use technology — they are trained to build, test, protect and commercialise it for Malaysia.
The Tarbiyat model is designed as a full residential, mission-driven campus that also functions as a youth R&D institution, an innovation laboratory and an IP-commercialisation pathway. The aim is simple: a student should leave with recognised academic credentials, but also with verified evidence of systems, prototypes and solutions actually built over the years.
Mission Labs instead of ordinary school laboratories — professional-grade environments for AI and machine learning, robotics, cybersecurity, electric mobility, energy systems, digital fabrication, drones and other frontier technologies.
Real national missions — students work on carefully selected problems brought by government agencies, universities, government-linked companies and industry partners, under secure and supervised conditions.
Proof of capability, not marks alone — national examinations remain the formal academic safety net, while a digital Proof of Capability Profile records what each student can actually design, build and solve.
A youth IP and micro-venture pathway — promising student work can move from prototype to patent, licensing, startup or government deployment under Malaysian law and a Government-approved IP policy.
Sovereign technology and data architecture — sensitive student data, mission records and government/industry R&D can be held on Malaysian-controlled infrastructure with strict access and cybersecurity rules.
A residential culture of discipline, mentoring and national purpose — the 24-hour model means an integrated campus life of study, laboratories, mentoring, wellbeing, sport, teamwork and character formation; it does not mean 24-hour classroom teaching.
This is a long-term national capability project, not a short-term school reform. If implemented well and linked to Malaysian institutions, its value would be measured by what its students and alumni create for the country. The intended outcomes are:
A continuous pipeline of young engineers, makers, researchers and technology founders who begin serious project work years before university.
More Malaysian-owned prototypes, patents, software, hardware solutions and licensable intellectual property in areas such as AI, robotics, cyber systems, energy and advanced manufacturing.
A practical import-substitution pathway: selected technologies currently purchased from abroad can become student-industry missions for local redesign, adaptation and eventual production.
A stronger bridge between school, university, government and industry — with companies and public institutions helping define real missions rather than appearing only for occasional seminars.
A visible Malaysian model for youth technology development that can, after proof of success, be replicated nationally and potentially presented as a Malaysian-led innovation model for the wider region.
The school should belong to Malaysia, not to Tarbiyat.
The proposed institution would be Government-owned and governed. The Government of Malaysia would determine the site, legal structure, admissions policy, curriculum recognition, security framework, budget, national mission priorities and the rules governing student and national-project intellectual property. No private ownership of the school is sought by Tarbiyat Sociotech Foundation.
Tarbiyat Sociotech Foundation proposes to work under Government direction to adapt the model for Malaysia, prepare the detailed implementation framework, define the laboratory and campus specifications, map the academic and mission system, establish operating protocols, support recruitment and training, build initial government/industry mission partnerships, and assist the school through commissioning and an agreed long-term stabilisation period. The objective would be to develop Malaysian leadership and operating capacity from the beginning, with increasing day-to-day responsibility transferred to the national team as the institution matures.
At this stage, we are not seeking approval of a final budget or asking Malaysia to accept a finished foreign blueprint. We respectfully seek the opportunity to present the concept, understand Malaysia's priorities, and — if the Government sees merit — prepare a Malaysia-specific feasibility and sovereign implementation framework for formal evaluation.
